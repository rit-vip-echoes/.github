# **Music Composition Guide**
Written By Ryan Keller

Digital composition and game composition are two incredibly and increasingly diverse fields, so this ever-growing document is meant to reflect that. As better resources for composition are released, this document should aim to stay on the forefront for the good of the next sound designers on the project.

## Software Guide

If you can get access to SAU A406, you can use the paid ProTools for free with a very nice keyboard for input.

### [Musescore](https://musescore.org/en)
Great for scoring things out if that’s how you’re used to thinking about music. I used to recommend only using soundfonts that didn’t try to be an orchestral instrument due to usual MIDI limitations, but the fonts you can get through the MuseScore4 MuseHub actually do a pretty decent job if you work with dynamics and mixer a bit.

References:

[1](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dIMFQ7s31DM) - Good for beginners to scoring. Some tips in the back half might be useful for those new to the tool even with past scoring knowledge, but they’re nothing too unintuitive.

[2](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nZ8ekiMNgtw) - A short but enlightening guide to working with the MuseScore4 mixer, which is really important for getting professional sounds out of the software.

[3](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uLjwD4uxa0Y) - This just shows you how to access note velocity, which alongside the mixer is how you can really sculpt your sound. Definitely worth the minute or two if you’re unfamiliar with how to get to the velocity setting.

[4](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5p6KLjMSna8) - Although largely geared towards orchestral sounds, this video is full of cool little tips for getting some sounds to the next level. If this may interest you, please listen at 1.25x or 1.5x speed for at least most of the video.

### [Soundation](https://soundation.com/)
Does an excellent job of recreating the feel of ProTools in a free, online environment. Aside from the more standard DAW features, this has another massive leg up over MuseScore in terms of its synth spread. You’re also able to very easily import your own drum/percussion/anything sounds and use the free beatmaker to make tracks out of them. This also works well with other DAWS; for example, if you’re using MuseScore for something orchestral and want to mix the entire exported track, you can drop it into Soundation and mix with the layering more easily. Their educational license (same as the personal one) clears us to use it in the VIP.

[Soundation Tutorials](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL37DH0NGvIhH4adMeihTlj91la5ylN8IB)

### [SoundTrap](https://www.soundtrap.com/musicmakers)
Is similar to Soundation, but with more of an emphasis on loops. While it has a lower barrier of entry thanks to the loops it provides and the simplified GUI, it also removes a lot of the functionality, which spawns several trade-offs you’d get for choosing this over Soundation. That said, the bottom line is that this is a great tool for whipping up a simple, polished beat with decent synth work. Their educational license (same as the personal one) clears us to use it in the VIP.

[SoundTrap Tutorials](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLQXQvkfg99q6_yMB3bkm1vDTDaZlrHeAn)

### Soundful and AI
Soundful (and other AI music generators/DAWs) - Soundful is one of the primary AI-led music production DAWs at the time of this document’s inception. While it might be a useful tool for spitballing, AI shouldn’t be used to create any finished tracks for this project.

## Composition Guide
### Relevant Music Theory

[This playlist](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL1302D94F247600CD) was created by somebody outside of the team, but has a lot of great information for getting started with music theory all the way up to chord inversions and suspended chords.

[This playlist](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PLH1Kp5ewZe_TP3CMB6LBw9ZjRCduyqoqj) is another one by a creator, and has a lot of different topics, making it a great compliment / second step for the one above. Some of the topics include modes, countermelody, and secondary dominants.

### Game Composition
The theory behind layering is fairly important for game music. Horizontal layering gives you a way to transition to new sections, whereas vertical layering ensures you can add some variance behind the section’s main motif. Here are some solid intro videos (only 20 minutes total):

[View the playlist](https://youtube.com/playlist?list=PL4pNKOWSpa5Xpib_ioOBoPwK9S-Uy0YeX&si=MF1Eopw2p10gYstW)

[Add to the playlist](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL4pNKOWSpa5Xpib_ioOBoPwK9S-Uy0YeX&jct=Nqhd80978E2Sox8YkdEmEA)

[This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8msUq2njAnI) is a great and short reference for some theory behind creating character motifs which can be applied to creating really any vibe you’re envisioning.

### Adding Emotions
[This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9RP3KQHhYBY) is a great one for the theory behind happy music. The first 10 minutes are the most valuable as they give you some solid theory for writing happy music, and the next 15 show him sculpting a tune using that theory.

[This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MKl_SUwPqJY) is another great Guy Michelmore video about a few tricks for writing sad music. While not comprehensive, the section from 4:30-6:30 is a great (and very short watch) to get some good ideas.

[This](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8hI94aYUPM8) is a reference for “liminal space” music, some concepts of which can be stolen to make things skew more dreamlike or unsettling. This tutorial isn’t great on theoretical explanations, but the demo can give some ideas on techniques and sounds to use.
